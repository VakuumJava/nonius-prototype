Dividend withholding tax: the basics across borders

Why a foreign dividend often arrives smaller than announced, what treaty rates and reclaims are, and why your tax adviser should have the final word.

A company announces a dividend of a certain amount per share. When it reaches your account, the figure is often lower. If the company is based in a different country from you, the difference is usually withholding tax: tax taken by the company's home country before the money ever leaves it.

This article explains the mechanics in general terms. Tax rules differ by country, change over time and depend on your personal situation, so treat what follows as background for a conversation with a tax adviser or your tax authority, not as a guide to your own tax return.

What withholding is

Many countries tax dividends paid to foreign shareholders at the source. The paying company, or the bank that handles the payment, deducts the tax and passes it to the authorities there. You receive the net amount. The country where you live may then tax the same dividend again as part of your income.

To avoid taxing the same income twice in full, many pairs of countries have double taxation treaties. A treaty may limit the rate the source country can withhold to below its normal statutory rate. Whether you get the lower rate straight away, or only after filing a reclaim, depends on the countries involved, your broker and the paperwork you have provided, such as a certificate of tax residence.

An illustrative example

The numbers below are invented and round on purpose; they do not describe any real country.

  • A gross dividend of 100 is declared.
  • The source country withholds 30 at its statutory rate, and you receive 70.
  • Under the treaty between the two countries, the rate is limited to 15, so 15 of the 30 may be reclaimable from the source country, through a procedure that can take months.
  • Your home country taxes the dividend and may allow the 15 that the source country was entitled to keep as a credit against your own tax on it.

In this example, the money withheld beyond the treaty rate is not lost, but it does not come back by itself either. Whether reclaiming it is practical depends on the amounts, the procedure and its costs.

The dividend that arrives is not the dividend that was declared, and the difference is not always final.

Funds add another layer

When you hold a fund rather than the shares directly, withholding can happen at two points in the chain: once when companies pay dividends to the fund, and again when the fund distributes to you, depending on where the fund is domiciled. Two funds tracking the same index but domiciled in different countries can therefore lose different amounts to tax along the way. Accumulating funds, which reinvest dividends, are still subject to withholding at the first step.

How Nonius records dividends

Nonius records each dividend at the net amount that reached your account, which is the money you actually have. Where a broker reports the withheld tax separately, it is kept next to the payment so that you can see it. Nonius does not calculate what you owe, does not know your treaty position and does not file reclaims. For all of that, your tax adviser or your tax authority is the right place to ask.
