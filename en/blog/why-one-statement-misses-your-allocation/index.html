Why one broker statement never shows your allocation

Each statement shows one account. Cash at the bank, a pension, a wallet and what your funds hold stay outside it, and so does your real allocation.

A broker statement is precise about one thing: what sits in that account on that date. It is easy to read it as a picture of your investments as a whole. For most people it is not, and the gap is often large enough to change how risky the whole looks.

What a single statement leaves out

List where the money of a typical investing household actually sits, and the list grows quickly:

  • Cash in an everyday bank account or a savings account, which is part of your allocation even if it earns little.
  • A workplace or private pension, often the largest single asset, reported once or twice a year by a different provider.
  • A crypto wallet, held at an exchange or in self-custody, with no link to the broker at all.
  • A second broker, kept for a savings plan or for a market the first one does not offer.
  • Property, a private loan or an employee share plan, which no statement covers.

Each of these changes the shares of the whole. A brokerage account that is 90% equities may belong to someone whose total wealth is less than half in equities once the pension and the cash are counted. The reverse happens too: a cautious-looking account can sit next to a large, concentrated position somewhere else.

The two rulers use generated demo data. The first shows one brokerage account by asset class. The second shows the same household with every account added: the bank balance, a crypto wallet and a workplace pension. The brokerage account has not changed, yet the picture has.

The same is true of currency. A brokerage account full of euro-denominated funds can look free of currency risk, while a pension invested worldwide and a crypto wallet priced in dollars add exposure that no single statement reports. Region and sector shift in the same way once every account is counted.

Your allocation is a property of everything you own, not of the account you happen to be looking at.

Funds hide a second layer

Even inside one account, the label on a holding can mislead. A global equity fund is one line on the statement but owns shares across many countries, sectors and currencies. Two funds with different names can hold many of the same companies. Reading the allocation of a portfolio of funds means looking through each fund to what it holds, which is usually called look-through. Without it, a portfolio of five funds can be far less diversified than five lines suggest.

Putting the pieces together

The practical answer is to collect every account in one place and read the shares from the total. That is what Nonius is built for: brokerage, bank, pension and crypto accounts in one ledger, in one base currency, with allocation shown by asset class, region, sector and currency. Accounts that cannot be connected can be imported from a CSV export or entered by hand, and they count in the totals like any other. Then a figure such as the share of equities means what it should: a share of everything you have.

Whatever tool you use, the habit is the same. Before deciding whether a portfolio is too risky or too cautious, make sure you are looking at the whole of it, including the parts that never appear on a broker statement.
