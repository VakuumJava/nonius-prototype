Time-weighted vs money-weighted return, worked through

Two correct numbers can describe the same year very differently. A worked example shows what each return measures and when to read which one.

Open two portfolio tools on the same account and they may disagree about how last year went. Often neither is wrong. One reports a time-weighted return, the other a money-weighted return, and the two answer different questions. Knowing which question you are asking saves a lot of confusion.

Two questions, two numbers

The time-weighted return asks how well the investments did, regardless of when you added or took out money. It cuts the year into periods at every deposit or withdrawal, measures the return of each period on its own, and chains them together. Your timing has no influence on the result, which is why fund managers report it: they do not decide when their clients pay in.

The money-weighted return asks how well your money did. It is the internal rate of return of all your cash flows: the single annual rate that turns everything you paid in into what you hold now. Periods in which more of your money was invested count for more. Your timing is part of the result, for better or worse.

A worked example

The numbers below are illustrative, chosen to keep the arithmetic readable. You start the year with €10,000. By the end of June the portfolio has grown to €11,000. On 1 July you add another €10,000, so €21,000 is invested for the second half of the year, which then falls by 5%.

PeriodValue at startDepositValue at endReturn
Jan–Jun€10,000—€11,000+10.0%
Jul–Dec€21,000€10,000€19,950−5.0%

The time-weighted return chains the two periods: 1.10 × 0.95 = 1.045, so the year comes out at +4.5%. The deposit does not enter the calculation at all; it only marks the point where one period ends and the next begins.

The money-weighted return looks for the rate r at which €10,000 growing for a full year plus €10,000 growing for half a year add up to €19,950. Solving 10,000 × (1 + r) + 10,000 × √(1 + r) = 19,950 gives r ≈ −0.3%. You paid in €20,000 and now hold €19,950, so a small loss is exactly what you would expect.

The investments gained 4.5% over the year; the money you put in lost 0.3%. Both statements are true.

Which one to read

Use the time-weighted figure to judge the investments themselves: a fund, a strategy, an allocation, or your portfolio against an index. Because deposits are neutralised, a large transfer never looks like a good month, and the comparison with a benchmark stays fair.

Use the money-weighted figure to judge your own experience, including the effect of when you added money. In the example the gap comes entirely from timing: twice as much money was exposed to the weaker half of the year. A gap between the two numbers is not an error; it is information about your cash flows.

If you never add or withdraw money, the two numbers are the same. The more irregular your deposits, the further apart they can drift, which is why a portfolio fed by a monthly savings plan and the occasional larger transfer is worth reading both ways.

Two common traps

  • Annualising a short period. A money-weighted return over a few weeks, scaled up to a full year, can show extreme values that mean very little.
  • Comparing across methods. A fund factsheet reports a time-weighted figure; set it against your own time-weighted return, not against the money-weighted one.
